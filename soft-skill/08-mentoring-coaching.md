# Mentoring and Coaching

## Context

Investing in other people's growth is one of the highest-leverage activities available to senior engineers and engineering leaders. A senior engineer who grows three mid-level engineers creates more value than a senior engineer who writes 3× the code.

Most engineers default to mentoring (sharing expertise) when coaching (unlocking the mentee's own thinking) would produce better long-term outcomes. Understanding the difference is the starting point.

**The three roles**:
| Role | What it is | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Mentor** | Share experience and knowledge; advise based on what worked for you | When someone lacks domain knowledge you have; onboarding; career navigation |
| **Coach** | Ask questions to help someone solve their own problems; develop their thinking | When someone is capable but stuck; building judgment and autonomy |
| **Sponsor** | Use your social capital to create visibility and opportunity for someone | When someone is ready for the next level but lacks access |

Most relationships need all three at different times. The mistake is using mentoring (advice-giving) when coaching (question-asking) would serve the person better.

## The GROW Model for Coaching

When coaching, structure the conversation using GROW:

```
GOAL      What does the person want to achieve in this conversation?
          "What would a useful outcome from our conversation look like for you?"

REALITY   What is the current situation? What have they already tried?
          "What's happening right now? What have you tried?"
          "What's getting in the way?"

OPTIONS   What are the possible paths forward?
          "What could you do? What have you not tried yet?"
          "If you knew you couldn't fail, what would you do?"
          "What would [someone they admire] do?"

WILL      What are they going to do, specifically?
          "What are you going to do and by when?"
          "How confident are you? What would make it 9/10 instead of 7/10?"
```

**GROW principles**:
- Spend 70% of the conversation in O and W, not G and R.
- Resist the urge to move directly to Options — insufficient time in Reality produces weak options.
- End every conversation with a specific commitment, not an intent.

## Socratic Coaching: Questions Before Answers

The single most powerful technique in coaching is asking questions before sharing your own view.

**Why it works**:
- The person develops their own reasoning capability instead of borrowing yours
- The answer they arrive at is one they believe — not one they're complying with
- You often learn something about the problem when you listen carefully to their answer

**Coaching question bank**:

| Purpose | Questions |
|---|---|
| Understand the real problem | "What are you really trying to solve here?" / "What would success look like?" |
| Surface assumptions | "What are you assuming must be true?" / "What would change your view?" |
| Generate options | "What else could you do?" / "What haven't you considered yet?" |
| Build self-awareness | "What worked? What didn't?" / "What would you do differently?" |
| Deepen accountability | "What specifically will you do, and by when?" / "What might get in the way?" |
| Reframe the situation | "If the constraint wasn't [X], what would you do?" |

**The "tell" trap**: If you catch yourself thinking "I know the answer and they're almost there — I'll just tell them," wait one more question. The value is in the final step of their own reasoning.

## Career Conversations

A career conversation is not a performance review. It is a forward-looking investment in someone's growth.

**Three-part career conversation framework**:

```
1. Life history (the foundation)
   Ask about what has energised them most — in work, education, personal life.
   Not to pry: to understand what patterns of engagement actually drive them.
   "Tell me about a time in your career where you felt most alive — what were you doing?"

2. Dreams (the direction)
   Ask what they want, without anchoring to their current role.
   "If you could do any job — knowing you'd be good at it — what would it be?"
   "What do you want your career to look like in 5 years? 10?"
   Document this. Revisit it in future conversations.

3. 18-month plan (the next step)
   Translate the long-term direction into achievable near-term growth.
   "Given where you want to go, what skills would most accelerate that?"
   "What can you do in this role in the next 18 months that would build toward that?"
   "What can I do to help?"
```

## Sponsorship

Mentoring and coaching remain between you and the mentee. Sponsorship uses your reputation and relationships to create opportunity.

**Sponsorship actions**:
- Nominate someone for a challenging project in a meeting where they're not present
- Say someone's name to a hiring manager or a senior leader
- Invite someone to a decision-making meeting and make space for their voice
- Write a specific, evidence-based recommendation, not generic praise
- Give credit explicitly when reporting on work they led

**Sponsorship vs advocacy**:
- Advocacy: "I think Maria is great."
- Sponsorship: "Maria led the incident investigation on the payment outage and built the post-mortem process that's now adopted across all 4 teams. She should be in the room when we're designing the resilience strategy."

## Onboarding Ownership

The people most responsible for a new engineer's success are their team members and their tech lead / buddy, not HR. Poor onboarding causes attrition within the first 6 months more than almost anything else.

**First 90 days structure**:

| Phase | Goal | Activities |
|---|---|---|
| **Days 1–14** | Safety and orientation | Know the tools; push one change to production; meet key people |
| **Days 15–45** | First ownership | Lead a small feature end-to-end; run a PR review; shadow an incident |
| **Days 46–90** | Growing autonomy | Identify and fix a piece of tech debt; propose a small improvement; present in team meeting |

**Buddy responsibilities**:
- Check in daily in the first week, weekly after that
- Answer "stupid questions" without judgment — there are no stupid questions during onboarding
- Surface the unwritten norms (how decisions really get made; who to talk to for what; culture quirks)
- Make the introduction network explicit: "You should know [person] who does [thing]"

## Anti-Patterns in Mentoring and Coaching

| Anti-pattern | What it looks like | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **Just tell them the answer** | Skip the thinking process; hand over the solution | They solve this problem once; they don't learn to solve the next one |
| **Mentoring dependency** | They only work when you're available | Doesn't build autonomy; scales to one person |
| **Career projection** | Assume they want what you wanted | The path you took may be wrong for them |
| **Generic praise** | "Great job on that!" | Doesn't reinforce the behaviour; doesn't help them repeat the success |
| **Avoiding difficult feedback** | Protecting them from hard truths | They receive the feedback from someone (or something) less caring later |
| **Sponsoring without evidence** | Vouching for someone on vague positive impressions | Harms your credibility; may harm them too if they're set up to fail |

## Self-Assessment Checklist

```
Coaching
  [ ] I ask at least 3 questions before offering my own view in coaching conversations
  [ ] I use GROW structure (or equivalent) in 1:1 coaching conversations
  [ ] I end coaching conversations with a specific commitment, not a "let's see"

Career development
  [ ] I know the long-term career direction of each person I am developing
  [ ] I have had a dedicated career conversation (not a check-in) in the last quarter
  [ ] I can name one specific opportunity I have created for each person this quarter

Mentoring and sponsorship
  [ ] I distinguish when someone needs advice (mentor) vs needs to think (coach)
  [ ] I have actively sponsored at least one person's visibility in the last 6 months
  [ ] My feedback is specific and behavioural, not generic or vague

Onboarding
  [ ] New team members have a structured 90-day plan with milestones
  [ ] I have introduced new engineers to the informal network and unwritten norms
```
